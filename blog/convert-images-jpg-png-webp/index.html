How to convert images between JPG, PNG and WebP

Published 21 November 2026 · SnapFixy team · 5 minute read

Sooner or later every image ends up in the wrong format. A form only accepts JPG, and your file is a PNG. A website wants lightweight WebP, and yours is a five-megabyte JPG. A design tool needs a transparent PNG, and the logo you have is a flat JPG with a white box behind it. Converting an image — changing its format without changing what it shows — is a thirty-second job, once you know which format to pick.

The conversion itself is simple. The decision is the hard part, because the three common formats make different trade-offs between quality, file size and features. Get the choice wrong and you lose transparency, or quality, or both.

Why three formats exist

Formats exist because no single one is best at everything. PNG is lossless and supports transparency, which makes it perfect for logos and screenshots — and large for photographs. JPG throws away fine detail your eye barely notices, which makes photographs much smaller — but it cannot hold transparency. WebP is the modern compromise from Google: smaller than JPG at the same quality, with transparency like PNG, and accepted by nearly every current browser. The right format is the one that fits where the image is going.

The three formats at a glance

  • PNG — lossless, every pixel preserved exactly, supports transparency. Best for logos, screenshots, diagrams and anything with text. Costs larger files for photographs.
  • JPG (JPEG) — the long-standing standard for photographs. Discards fine detail for much smaller files. No transparency: transparent areas become white.
  • WebP — modern and compact, smaller than JPG at the same quality, supports transparency. Nearly all current browsers accept it, though a few older apps do not.

How to convert an image, step by step

Your files are never uploaded. The conversion is done by your browser itself — the image never leaves your device.

  1. Open the image converter. It works in any modern browser, on a computer or a phone.
  2. Drop your image in or click to browse. PNG, JPG, WebP, GIF, BMP and most other formats work.
  3. Pick the format you need. PNG for lossless quality and transparency, JPG for the smallest photographs, WebP for the modern web.
  4. Set the quality for JPG or WebP. The default of 92% keeps photos looking crisp.
  5. Convert, compare with the original, and download the result in its new format.

Transparency: the rule that catches everyone out

The single most common conversion mistake is losing transparency. JPG cannot store it, so converting a transparent PNG to JPG fills the empty areas with white — and there is no way back. If the image needs to stay transparent, convert to PNG or WebP instead. And remember that converting the other way does not help either: turning a JPG into a PNG only changes the container. The white background that was baked into the JPG is still there, because converting removes nothing — to actually take a background out, use the background remover.

Quality settings that matter

Converting to PNG is lossless, so quality is never a question. Converting to JPG or WebP re-encodes the image, so keep the quality high — 90% or above — if the result needs to look identical. Lower settings save space, but the softening shows first in fine detail: hair, text, distant trees. And never convert a JPG to another JPG at a lower quality and then back again — every round throws away a little more, and the damage adds up.

WebP deserves a special mention here, because it behaves slightly differently from JPG. At the same quality setting, a WebP file is usually smaller than the equivalent JPG, and it keeps transparency where JPG cannot. If the destination accepts WebP — most websites and modern apps do — it is often the best of both worlds. The exception is older software: if the file will be opened in an old desktop app or a legacy system, stick with JPG or PNG, which nothing refuses.

Common mistakes

  • Converting a JPG to PNG expecting it to get sharper. It keeps exactly the quality the JPG had, and the file gets bigger.
  • Saving a transparent image as JPG. Transparency is lost instantly.
  • Converting an animated GIF and expecting the animation to survive. The converter reads the first frame and saves it as a still image.
  • Converting when compression is the real problem. If file size is the issue, the image compressor is the better tool.

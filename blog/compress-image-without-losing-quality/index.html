How to compress an image without losing quality

Published 9 November 2026 · SnapFixy team · 4 minute read

"Compress this image, but don't lose quality" sounds like a contradiction — and strictly speaking, lossy compression does discard information. But here's the useful truth: most images carry far more data than the human eye can actually see. Compress well, and a file can shrink by 70–90% while looking, to any normal viewer, exactly the same. Here's how to do it deliberately rather than by luck.

First, understand the two levers: dimensions and quality

File size is driven mainly by two things. The first is dimensions — how many pixels the image has. A 4000-pixel-wide phone photo has sixteen times the pixels of a 1000-pixel-wide web image, and pixels are what you're storing. If an image will only ever be shown at 1000px wide, keeping it at 4000px is pure waste; resizing it down is the single biggest, safest saving you can make, using a tool like our image resizer.

The second lever is quality — how aggressively the format discards fine detail when saving. This is the slider on every compressor, and it deserves more respect than it gets.

The quality sweet spot

For photographs saved as JPG or WebP, the range between about 75% and 85% is where the magic happens. At 100%, the encoder keeps detail nobody can see and the file balloons. At 80%, the file is often a fifth of the size — and side by side, most people genuinely cannot tell which is which. Drop below 60% and you start to see it: blocky patches in skies, fuzzy edges around text, smeared detail in hair and foliage.

The professional habit is simple: compress at 80%, look at the result at the size it will actually be displayed, and only then decide. Our image compressor is built around exactly this loop — it shows the original and the compressed version together, with both file sizes, so you judge with your eyes rather than guessing. If it looks soft, nudge the quality up and run it again; experimenting costs nothing.

Choose the right format for the job

  • Photographs → JPG or WebP. WebP usually wins on size at the same visual quality; JPG wins on universal compatibility. Either compresses photos beautifully.
  • Graphics, logos and screenshots → PNG. PNG is lossless, so text and sharp edges stay crisp. But don't expect miracles on file size — and never save a photograph as PNG unless you need transparency.
  • Transparency needed → PNG or WebP. JPG cannot do transparency at all. If you're unsure which you need, our comparison of PNG vs JPG vs WebP lays it out with examples of each trade-off.

Habits that keep quality high

  • Always compress from the original. Re-compressing an already-compressed JPG stacks the losses. Keep your camera originals untouched and make compressed copies from them.
  • Compress once, at the end. Do your resizing, cropping and converting first, then a single compression pass as the final step.
  • Match the tool to the display size. A thumbnail doesn't need print resolution. Ask "how big will this actually be seen?" and size for that.
  • Zoom in on the tricky areas. Skies, skin and shadows show compression artefacts first. Check those, not the busy detailed parts — detail hides a multitude of sins.

What "without losing quality" honestly means

If every pixel must be preserved — archival scans, medical or legal imagery — use PNG and accept the size, or keep the original file and compress a copy for everyday use. For everything else — websites, listings, emails, social posts, documents — "no visible loss" is the standard that matters, and it's entirely achievable. The test is never the numbers; it's whether a person looking at the image, at its real display size, can tell. Compress at sensible settings, and they can't.
